123 Reg data breach: what the AI-driven attack means for you

123 Reg says an AI-driven attack read customer data on 18 September 2026. This page lists what was exposed, what is not known and the checks for your account.

Bamboo Flags30 Oct 20263 min read

On 28 October 2026, The Register reported that 123 Reg told its customers about a data breach. 123 Reg is a UK domain registrar and web host that GoDaddy owns. The intrusion occurred on 18 September 2026. The company calls it "the first known AI-driven cyberattack against our company."

If your business has a domain, email or a website at 123 Reg, this applies to you. The data that the company lists does not include passwords or payment details. It is the data that makes a fake message from 123 Reg look real.

What data the attackers read

The notification email lists contact information, "related customer relationship data", company VAT numbers and product information from an API. A company spokesperson gave The Register a longer list:

  • names and phone numbers
  • country, language and currency preferences
  • the date when the account was created
  • the products on the account
  • marketing preferences

The email says that the activity "was limited to reading" customer data. The spokesperson said that it is "possible they created copies of the information." This is why 123 Reg sent the notification.

Why the data matters for phishing

With this data, an attacker can send a message that names your company, your VAT number and the domains or hosting plan that you pay for. A fake renewal notice or a fake "account problem" call can then look correct.

In South Korea, after the bank attacks described below, the Financial Services Commission and the Financial Supervisory Service warned consumers about phishing and loan scams that used the leaked data, according to The Hacker News.

123 Reg tells customers to treat unexpected messages from the company with caution. To check a message, go directly to the 123 Reg website and log in. Do not use the links, phone numbers or contact details in the message. The company says that it will "never contact you to request your password, payment details, or verification codes."

What 123 Reg says about the attackers

According to The Register, 123 Reg says that the core attacker IP addresses were in China and Singapore, "indicating a strong Chinese nexus." It also found AI attack "skills" on Chinese servers, including copies of skills that Anthropic published as open source.

123 Reg suspects a link to the attacks on South Korean financial companies. On 7 October 2026, CrowdStrike described that campaign. The attacker used ARTEX, "a recently released open-source agentic penetration testing (pentesting) tool developed in China", together with large language models. CrowdStrike assesses with moderate confidence that the actor is "likely a Chinese speaker and financially motivated." The developer of ARTEX said that the tool is moving to closed source.

123 Reg reported the attack to law enforcement and to data protection authorities in the UK and the EU. It also worked with Singapore internet providers to shut down attacker servers.

What is not known

  • 123 Reg did not say how many customers are affected.
  • The link to China and to the ARTEX campaign is the company's assessment. No authority has confirmed it.
  • No source says how the attackers got access, or how the AI tools were used in the attack.
  • "First known AI-driven cyberattack" is the company's own description.

Earlier this week we described the PHP2Shell vulnerability, a different attack on the web servers that small businesses use.

Checks for your 123 Reg account

  1. Turn on two-step verification in your 123 Reg account. The notification email says that the setup instructions are in the account.
  2. Tell the people who pay invoices or answer the phone about the breach. Give them one rule: no password, payment details or codes by email, text or phone.
  3. Do not click links in messages about renewals, payments or account problems. Log in on the 123 Reg website and check there.
  4. Write down which products you have at 123 Reg. A message that names the wrong product is fake, but a message that names the right one is not proof that it is real.
  5. Forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk, the National Cyber Security Centre reporting address.
  6. If someone already answered a fake message, follow the NCSC steps for what to do if you shared information. If you lost money, report it to Report Fraud.
