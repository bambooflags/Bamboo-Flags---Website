AI agents and bank passwords: what US banks said in October 2026

Bank of America, Chase and Schwab told customers not to give bank passwords to AI agents. This article lists what they said, who is liable, and what to check.

Bamboo Flags28 Oct 20264 min read

On 27 October 2026, TechCrunch reported an email from Bank of America to its customers. The email tells customers not to share their username and password with another person or outside service, "including an AI-enabled tool or agent that offers to act on your behalf." It also says: "keep in mind that you're responsible for activity resulting from that access." A Bank of America spokesperson confirmed the email to TechCrunch.

If an AI agent in your business logs in to a bank account with your password, this applies to you. The bank treats the agent's actions as your actions.

What the banks found

On 22 October, Axios reported that large US banks detect AI agents that the banks did not vet inside customer accounts. CBS News summarised the report on 23 October. According to Madison Mills, the Axios reporter, customers often give their username or password to the agent, which is against the bank's terms of service. Some agents did things that the user did not ask for. One agent called a bank with a complaint without asking the account holder first.

Mills also said that the agents did not hack accounts or take money. They were "largely following user instructions." Decrypt reports that, according to Axios, one bank executive said it is hard to tell an agent that the customer approved from a rogue agent.

What each bank said

  • Chase: a spokesperson told CBS News that "Customers should not give their username and passwords to anyone." Chase supports data sharing that the customer approves through open banking providers.
  • JPMorgan: on the earnings call on 13 October, CEO Jamie Dimon said that the bank will not let just any outside agent inside the company.
  • Charles Schwab: on the earnings call on 15 October, CEO Rick Wurster said that Schwab blocks outside agents that use client login credentials or browser automation, according to American Banker. He said that other AI tools will be able to trade later, but not "move money, because we think there's too much risk there to the client and to the firm."
  • Morgan Stanley: a spokesperson said that the bank has not seen "rogue AI agents accessing our platforms."

On Bloomberg Television on 16 October, Wurster asked: "We can't control it, the client can't control it, something could go wrong and who's liable?" The Next Web reports that Schwab will build MCP servers for this. MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a standard way to connect an AI model to a system. Through these servers, an outside AI tool can check balances and do research, but it cannot move money.

Meta's Muse is the agent that the coverage names most often. We described Muse for Small Business in an earlier article.

Read-only access is the likely direction

According to Decrypt, most banks allow or plan to allow read-only access for agents. An agent with read-only access can see the account data, but it cannot make a payment. Budgeting apps already work this way. David Sacks, the White House AI adviser, gave the same model on X, quoted by CBS News: "Full write access (with user credentials) is a different risk category and should be approached carefully."

Who is liable on a business account

TechCrunch writes that at most banks, the account agreement makes the customer responsible for anyone who gets the login. It cites the U.S. Bank digital services agreement. That agreement says: "Any activity performed using your Login Credentials or your device will be presumed to be authorized by you." If you give your login credentials to a third party, "you are responsible for the use of your account."

For business accounts, the same agreement is stricter. For small business ACH payments that pass the bank's security procedure, "you will be obligated to pay us the amount of that ACH payment, even though you did not authorize" it. For wires, it says: "Your protections, and our liability, for unauthorized wire transfers are more limited than those for some other Digital Services." Consumer accounts refer to the protections of Regulation E. Your bank's terms can be different, so read them.

What is not known

  • No bank has published a list of agents that it approves or blocks.
  • The banks did not say how they detect an agent. Wurster said that Schwab stops outside agents "in the tens and twenties" each week, according to The Next Web.
  • No source reports money lost because of an agent.

Checks for your business bank accounts

  1. List each tool that logs in to a business bank account with a password. Include AI agents, bookkeeping tools and browser extensions.
  2. If you gave a bank password to an AI agent, change the password. The U.S. Bank agreement says that you must contact the bank to block access that you gave to a third party.
  3. Ask your bank which tools it supports through an official connection. Use that connection instead of a password, and choose read-only access if the tool only needs to see data.
  4. Do not let an agent use a browser profile that has saved bank passwords.
  5. For staff, use the sub-user or user management function of your bank, with limited permissions. Do not share one login.
  6. Read the part of your online banking agreement about login credentials and third parties. Check the limits for business wires and ACH payments.
