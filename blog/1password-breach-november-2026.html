1Password breach: customer emails taken, vaults not accessed

Attackers took 1Password source code and customer names and emails in November 2026. Vaults were not accessed. What changes for a business that uses 1Password.

Bamboo Flags27 Nov 20264 min read

On 25 November 2026, 1Password published a notice about a security incident. An attacker took private source code and some customer account data, such as names and sign-in email addresses. 1Password says the attacker never had access to customer vaults, where the stored passwords are kept.

According to TechCrunch, 1Password has more than 200,000 business customers. If your team uses it, the company says no action is needed in your account. The risk that remains is phishing: emails, texts or calls that use the stolen details to look real.

How the attacker got into 1Password

1Password began to investigate the attack on 19 November 2026. The attacker had access to the corporate GitHub account of one employee. GitHub is the service where the company stores its source code.

With that account, the attacker copied source code from private repositories and pushed malicious code to backend services. That code downloaded customer account data. The attacker also got into the CI/CD system, which builds and deploys the software and holds infrastructure secrets. 1Password says it rotated every credential that could have been exposed.

1Password has not said how the attacker got into the employee's account. It will publish a root cause analysis "in the coming weeks". It did not answer questions from TechCrunch or The Register.

What customer data the attacker took

According to the 1Password notice, the data of "a subset of users" includes:

  • names and email addresses
  • account and user identifiers, the account creation time and the last sign-in time
  • public keys, which are public by design, and SRP protocol parameters, which 1Password says cannot be used to open an account
  • tokens in some emailed invitation, provisioning and account recovery links
  • other internal data that is not secret, such as version numbers

1Password has not said how many customers are affected.

What 1Password says was not affected

The notice says that the attacker did not get vaults, vault items, the keys that protect vaults or account recovery keys. 1Password does not store account passwords or Secret Keys, so these were not in its systems. The apps and browser extensions were not affected.

A stolen token can complete an invitation or a recovery only together with a single sign-on (SSO) login or a code sent to the user's email. 1Password changed that code from six digits to ten letters and numbers, so it is harder to guess. A small number of users had an open invitation or recovery, for example a pending SCIM Bridge invitation. 1Password says it told them directly and found no account takeover with the tokens.

Why stolen source code matters

The Register compares this incident with the LastPass breach of 2022. In that case, an attacker first took source code from a developer's laptop. In a second attack on another employee's computer, the attacker got the keys to download the customer backup database. In December 2025, the UK data regulator fined LastPass £1.2 million. The breach affected up to 1.6 million UK users.

The two cases are not the same. 1Password says that it rolled back the affected services, removed the attacker's access and asked outside security experts to check its work. But the 1Password attacker had source code and access to the build system, and 1Password has not yet said how far that access went.

The risk for your team is phishing

The attacker now has names and work email addresses of 1Password users. That is enough for a fake "account recovery" or "invitation" email that looks real. 1Password says it "will never contact you to ask for your account password or Secret Key."

The company tells users to enter the account password and Secret Key only in the 1Password app or on the official website, after they open the app or type the address themselves. Send suspicious messages to support@1password.com. For other basic security steps, see our article on the NIST cybersecurity guide for small businesses.

What to do this week

  1. 01Tell everyone on your 1Password account about the incident.Say that no one needs to change the account password or the Secret Key.
  2. 02Warn your team about emails, texts and calls about 1Password invitations or account recovery.If no one in the business started the request, do not act on it.
  3. 03Open 1Password only from the app or by typing the address.Do not sign in through a link in an email.
  4. 04Check your 1Password admin settings for open invitations and recovery requests.Ask 1Password support about each one that no one in your team started.
  5. 05Read the root cause analysis when 1Password publishes it.Decide then if your business needs to take more steps.
