Cleverbridge breach: card numbers and CVV codes stolen

Cleverbridge, which sells software for Red Hat, Veeam and others, says attackers copied card data, CVV codes included. What to check as a buyer and as a seller.

Bamboo Flags25 Nov 20263 min read

On 4 November 2026, Cleverbridge detected unauthorized activity on its platform. The company later found that "a third party copied payment card data stored on our platform". It started to notify affected customers on 20 November. For some of them, the stolen data includes the CVV code, the three or four digits on the card that online shops ask for at checkout.

Cleverbridge runs the online shop, the payments and the subscriptions for software and SaaS companies. If your business bought software or a licence through one of these shops, a company card can be in the stolen data. If your business sells online through a payment platform, this incident shows what to ask that platform.

What Cleverbridge confirmed

The facts below come from the Cleverbridge incident update:

  • Cleverbridge took the affected systems offline on 4 November and brought them back online the same day.
  • The data that was copied includes "a small amount of payment card data". CVV data was included "in a minority of cases".
  • The incident was not ransomware. No systems were encrypted.
  • TransUnion's Cyberscout sends the notifications for Cleverbridge. Cleverbridge says these messages are real.
  • Affected cardholders get credit or identity monitoring where it is available.
  • Cleverbridge says that it never asks for card details, passwords or security codes by email, phone or text message.

The company says that it told data protection authorities, the card networks, its payment partners and a police cybercrime unit.

Which shops use Cleverbridge

BleepingComputer names Red Hat, SUSE, Tenable, Veeam, Jamf, Sony and Parallels as Cleverbridge customers. The Cleverbridge client stories page lists more. Cleverbridge has not said which of its clients' customers are affected, so a purchase from one of these companies does not mean that your card was stolen.

What the notices say, and what is not known

BleepingComputer saw one customer notice. It lists the card number, the expiry date and the CVV/CVC code. That notice says that no name, contact details or billing details were copied. The Register saw a different customer email, which also lists email addresses, billing addresses and phone numbers. The two reports do not agree, so read your own notice for the data that applies to you.

Cleverbridge has not said how the attackers got in, how long they had access or how many people are affected. It did not answer questions from BleepingComputer or The Register.

Why stolen CVV codes matter

The card industry rules, PCI DSS, do not allow a company to keep a CVV code after the payment is authorized. The PCI Security Standards Council says that this applies even if the data is encrypted, and that recurring payments and saved cards do not need the code. The same rule covers call recordings that contain card codes.

Cleverbridge has not explained whether it kept the codes after payment or whether the attackers captured them during checkout. The Register notes that the credit monitoring on offer does not stop payments with a stolen card, and suggests that affected customers ask for a new card.

The breach was at the platform, not at the shops

The software companies did not lose the data themselves. The platform that runs their checkout did, and it decides what card data it keeps. For another case where a third-party service put risk on many websites at once, see our article on the Optimizely hack.

What to do this week

  1. 01Search your email for the name Cleverbridge, and your card statements for software from the companies above.Note each company card that you used in a Cleverbridge shop.
  2. 02Read any notice from Cleverbridge or Cyberscout in full.Check which data it lists for your card, because the notices are not all the same.
  3. 03Ask your bank for a new card if the notice lists the CVV.Update the card in your subscriptions after the replacement arrives.
  4. 04Check company card statements each week.Report each payment that you do not recognise to the bank at once.
  5. 05Ignore calls, emails and text messages that ask for card details or codes.Cleverbridge says it never asks for them.
  6. 06Ask your own payment provider in writing if it ever stores CVV codes, and which data it would send in a breach notice.Keep the answer with your supplier records.
